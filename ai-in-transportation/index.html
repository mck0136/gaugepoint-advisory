01

What AI means in a transportation context

AI is useful when it improves a real operating decision or reduces the effort required to move information through a workflow. In transportation, that can mean reading a tender, forecasting capacity, identifying service risk, coordinating an exception, or verifying a physical event in a yard.

The important question is not which model is most advanced. It is whether the process, information, controls, and economics support a better outcome.

02

Automation, analytics, generative AI, agents, and applied AI

Automation

Executes a defined rule or repeatable sequence.

Analytics

Finds patterns, forecasts outcomes, and supports decisions.

Generative AI

Works with language and unstructured information to create, summarize, or retrieve content.

AI agents

Coordinate multi-step work across tools, people, and systems within defined authority.

Applied AI

Uses cameras, sensors, and intelligent analysis in physical operating environments.

03

High-value transportation use cases

Promising use cases tend to involve high-volume work, repeatable judgment, expensive delay, frequent exceptions, or information that is difficult to use at speed.

  • Rate and tender intake
  • Exception triage and status communication
  • Appointment and document follow-up
  • Pricing and customer profitability support
  • Forecasting and service-risk identification
  • Gate, asset, and condition verification

04

Why data quality alone is not enough

Accurate data matters, but a scalable workflow also needs ownership, timely access, system integration, decision rules, and a way to verify outputs. A clean data set does not resolve unclear accountability or a process that changes by branch.

05

Human-in-the-loop operations

Human-in-the-loop is an operating design choice, not a slogan. It defines what the system may recommend, what it may execute, when approval is required, what evidence a person sees, and how exceptions escalate.

AI agents should reduce waiting, chasing, repetitive follow-up, and administrative work without removing accountability.

06

Security, governance, and accountability

Governance should match the risk of the use case. It can include approved tools, role-based permissions, data boundaries, audit trails, output verification, escalation rules, vendor review, and a named owner for the business outcome.

07

How to build an AI business case

Start with baseline performance: labour effort, cycle time, error rate, service failure, lost revenue, or margin leakage. Estimate implementation effort and ongoing control costs. Then define the measurable change, adoption requirement, and time to value before selecting a solution.

08

Common implementation failures

  • Beginning with a vendor demo instead of an operating problem.
  • Automating an inconsistent process.
  • Ignoring system write-back and integration.
  • Leaving approval boundaries undefined.
  • Running a pilot without a baseline or scale decision.
  • Separating technology work from the employees who own the process.

09

How to determine organizational readiness

Assess strategic alignment, workflows, data, systems, people, governance, and economics together. The purpose is not to produce a decorative score. It is to determine where to act, what to fix first, and which opportunities deserve investment.
